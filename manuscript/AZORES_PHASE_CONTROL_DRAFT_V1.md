# Manuscript draft v1 — phase-specific control of European eel migration

## Working title

**Internal migratory readiness governs activation but attenuates during progression in European eel**

## Abstract

Migration emerges from the interaction between internal state and external opportunity, but whether their relative influence changes across sequential phases of a migration is rarely tested directly. European eel (*Anguilla anguilla*) provides an unusually tractable system because silvering stage offers an independently measured axis of migratory readiness, while telemetry resolves migration activation, timing and subsequent progression through heterogeneous routes.

We analysed public telemetry products from six European eel projects in which female Durif stages FIII–FV were available together with a common migration classifier. After preserving the source study's expert corrections, migration initiation increased from 59.0% in FIII to 77.9% in FIV and 87.4% in FV individuals. In models stratified by project and release year and adjusted for body length and release timing, the odds of migration initiation increased 2.08-fold per Durif-stage increment (95% CI 1.56–2.76). A stratified Cox analysis similarly showed earlier migration onset at more advanced stages (hazard ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.13–1.47).

The same internal-state gradient weakened after migration had begun. Among initiators, Durif stage did not provide a general advantage for migration completion (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.83–1.59) or post-initiation migration speed (ratio 0.983, 95% CI 0.852–1.134). A direct stacked two-phase model confirmed that the Durif-stage effect was stronger at initiation than at completion (ratio of odds ratios 1.81, 95% CI 1.15–2.84; phase interaction p=0.0099). Across projects, a coarse resistance score was unrelated to initiation but strongly negatively associated with completion after initiation, although this comparison is project-confounded. An independent 2026 Dutch consecutive-barrier study provides compatible evidence that post-activation progression depends on barrier-specific discharge opportunity, wind, lunar conditions and prior passage experience.

These results support a sequential view of migration in which internal readiness primarily regulates entry into the migratory state, whereas route-specific ecological opportunity increasingly filters realised progression. Separating activation from progression clarifies why producing migration-ready individuals is not equivalent to ensuring successful escapement, with direct implications for management of fragmented migration routes.

## Introduction

Migration is not a single behavioural event. To complete a migration, an animal must first enter a movement state, then progress through a landscape whose physical and ecological conditions can either permit or obstruct movement. Movement-ecology theory has long recognised that realised movement reflects the interaction among internal state, motion and navigation capacities, and external environment. Yet empirical studies commonly analyse migration departure, migration speed, barrier passage or final success as separate endpoints. This can obscure whether the same biological controls operate across all phases of the movement sequence.

European eel is well suited to separating these sequential controls. Before seaward migration, eels undergo silvering: a suite of morphological and physiological changes associated with transition from the continental growth phase to the reproductive migration phase. The Durif classification formalises this transition, with FIII representing a pre-migrant female stage and FIV–FV representing migrating stages. Silvering therefore provides an internal-state axis that is measured independently of subsequent telemetry-derived movement.

At the same time, realised seaward movement occurs through strongly heterogeneous routes. European eels may encounter free-flowing reaches, canals, pumps, weirs, shipping locks and tidal sluices, and migration timing and passage can depend on discharge, darkness, hydrodynamics and barrier operation. Thus the same individual can be highly migration-ready yet still experience delay or failure after movement has begun.

This distinction suggests a simple but under-tested prediction. If silvering primarily regulates the activation of migration, then Durif stage should strongly predict whether and when directed downstream movement begins. If post-activation progression becomes increasingly constrained by route opportunity, the general effect of Durif stage should weaken for migration speed and completion after initiation. The decisive test is therefore not whether stage is significant in one model and non-significant in another, but whether the effect of the same internal-state predictor differs directly across sequential movement phases.

We tested this prediction using public telemetry products from six European eel projects included in a recent Europe-wide migration synthesis. We reconstructed migration activation using the source study's fixed movement classifier and preserved its expert corrections. We then compared the effect of capture-time Durif stage on (1) migration initiation, (2) time to migration onset, (3) post-initiation speed and (4) completion conditional on initiation. A stacked two-phase model directly tested attenuation of the stage coefficient from initiation to completion. Finally, we used project-level resistance context as a non-causal bridge diagnostic and an independent Dutch consecutive-barrier study as an external ecological constraint on the post-activation interpretation.

We predicted that Durif stage would exert its strongest effect at migration activation, with weaker general influence after movement had begun.

## Results

### Advanced Durif stage strongly predicted migration initiation

Across six projects, 575 tracked females had exact FIII, FIV or FV classifications and compatible migration histories after preserving the source study's expert non-migrant corrections.

Migration initiation increased monotonically across stages:

- FIII: 154/261 (59.0%);
- FIV: 53/68 (77.9%);
- FV: 215/246 (87.4%).

After controlling for project × release year, within-stratum body length and within-stratum release timing, each one-stage increase in Durif classification was associated with a 2.08-fold increase in the odds of initiating migration (95% CI 1.56–2.76; p≈4.2×10^-7).

### More advanced stage predicted earlier movement-state expression

A stratified Cox model followed 570 individuals from release to first classified migration onset or censoring at the last telemetry observation. There were 418 migration-onset events across 13 project × release-year strata.

Each Durif-stage increment increased the migration-onset hazard by 28% (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.13–1.47; p=0.00016). Leave-one-project-out estimates remained positive in all cases (HR 1.09–1.52), although omission of the 2015 project widened the confidence interval through one.

### The Durif-stage advantage weakened after migration began

Among classified initiators, Durif stage no longer provided a clear general advantage for reaching the published successful-migrant endpoint. The adjusted odds ratio per stage increment was 1.15 (95% CI 0.83–1.59; p=0.412).

The same attenuation appeared for a continuous post-initiation metric. Migration speed differed little among stages after project-year, body-length and release-timing adjustment (multiplicative stage ratio 0.983, 95% CI 0.852–1.134; p=0.815).

### A direct phase interaction confirmed attenuation of internal-state control

A stacked continuation-ratio model directly estimated the Durif-stage coefficient for initiation and completion while clustering uncertainty by individual.

The stage effect was substantially stronger at initiation than at completion:

- initiation OR per stage: 2.08;
- completion OR per stage: 1.15;
- OR ratio: 1.81;
- 95% CI 1.15–2.84;
- phase interaction p=0.0099.

All six leave-one-project-out interaction estimates retained the same direction. Five remained conventionally supported at p<0.05; removing the 2015 project widened the interval through one.

### External route context aligned with completion but not initiation

Across the six projects, median WRS impact was essentially unrelated to migration-initiation rate (Spearman rho +0.029; exact permutation p=0.983).

In contrast, WRS was strongly negatively associated with completion conditional on initiation (rho -0.928; exact p=0.022). The negative rank relationship remained strong under leave-one-project-out analysis.

Because WRS is strongly confounded with project and tracking design, this result is interpreted only as bridge evidence that external route context aligns more closely with Gate 2 than Gate 1.

### An independent consecutive-barrier system constrained the Gate-2 interpretation

A 2026 Dutch study followed 40 FIII–FV European eels through a pumping station and subsequent tidal sluice. Thirty-five passed the pumping station and 27 completed seaward passage, with mean cumulative barrier delay of approximately 34 days.

Published passage models did not retain Durif stage as a generic predictor at the pumping station, and stage was non-identifiable at the tidal sluice because of confounding with body mass. Instead, passage and delay depended on barrier-specific variables including discharge-event duration, wind, lunar illumination and prior passage experience.

This independent system therefore provides ecological corroboration that post-activation progression is strongly filtered by route-specific opportunity rather than by a universal monotonic Durif advantage.

## Discussion spine

### 1. Migration contains sequential bottlenecks

The primary result is a phase difference, not merely a positive stage effect.

Silvering state strongly predicts entry into migration but its general predictive effect attenuates after activation.

### 2. Internal readiness is most informative before movement begins

The initiation and Cox results together indicate that advanced Durif stage predicts both whether and when directed migration is expressed.

Because FIII–FV are themselves biologically ordered readiness stages, this axis is treated as a positive-control measure of internal state rather than the novelty claim.

### 3. Progression becomes increasingly opportunity limited

Once movement begins, neither completion nor migration speed shows the same general Durif gradient.

Project-level resistance and the independent Dutch system both point toward route-specific conditions as increasingly important filters.

### 4. Why pooled migration success can obscure mechanism

A single final-success endpoint combines:

- readiness to initiate;
- timing of movement;
- hydrological opportunity;
- barrier passage;
- accumulated delay;
- endpoint observability.

Separating these steps reveals that the same covariate can be dominant at one stage and weak at another.

### 5. Conservation implication

Increasing the number of physiologically migration-ready silver eels is not equivalent to increasing escapement.

If post-activation progression is constrained by route opportunity, conservation must also maintain:

- adequate discharge windows;
- effective barrier passage;
- source-to-sea connectivity;
- low cumulative delay.

### 6. Limitations

- Europe-wide analyses are developmental and source data were inspected during hypothesis refinement.
- The movement classifier is inherited rather than re-tuned.
- Project context is strongly confounded with WRS.
- The Dutch system constrains but does not directly replicate the phase interaction.
- The analysis supports a shift in relative control, not an exclusive internal-to-external switch.
